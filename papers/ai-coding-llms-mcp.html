AI-Assisted Delivery, Connected Through MCP

A Case Study on AI in Product Development Flows: Testing Andrew Ng's theory of bottlenecks once engineering is expedited.

December 16, 2025

One product manager, one developer and one designer set out to cut the development lifecycle of a one-sprint feature to 30%, roughly three days, using AI-assisted coding. We tested this across three real features. The larger gain was not faster code. It was connecting the three disciplines through MCP tooling. The learnings were extended to discovery and GTM assets as separate experiments to prove we could extend further upstream and downstream.

Each feature was estimated at 0.9 to 1.1 sprints. A sprint runs 14 calendar days, which is 10 working days once the four weekend days are removed, or 80 hours at 8 hours a day. Fixed time takes 19.5 of those hours: TRR documentation (1.5), QA and bug fixes (8), internal dev team coordination (2) and scrum ceremonies or meetings (8). That leaves 60.5 hours for pure development. Anything left over was considered buffer.

At the time of this writing, QA had begun to use AI with product acceptance criteria from user stories to write automation tests and engineering had developed AI-agents that fixed bugs. These times would also display efficiency gains, though not recorded here.

As a regulated healthcare technology company, tools and data are not democratised beyond necessity. Privacy and security are imperative to how we collect, store, and port data.

1. Introduction

The question was never whether AI could write code faster. We knew it could. At the time I was curious about MCP. The question that interested me was whether AI could change how Product, Engineering and Design work together: turning a chain of repetitive reference and handoffs into one connected workflow.

This paper follows one product manager, one developer, and one designer, working independently and collaboratively, on the athenaOne Mobile platform at athenahealth as they tested that question across three real features.

Each feature is a separate section of the patient's chart and holds historical information that influences or advises treatment. I refer to them here as Feature A, Feature B and Feature C.

Each experiment was built on what the previous one exposed. The first made engineering faster and left Product and Design in their conventional streams. The second included Product. The third connected all three.

I then applied the learnings independently to two separate projects: discovery (a thematic analysis of user feedback) and GTM (communications that support a feature) to complete the hypothesis.

The Operational Cycle

I want to be clear about what this is. It is a small, single-team study on production-grade healthcare software. Every hour figure in this paper is an estimate. Baselines are the team’s planned estimates. AI-assisted figures are the team’s own recollection of time spent. None come from time-tracking tools.

Additionally, we operate in three quarterly releases. Each release comprises of 7-8 sprints, excluding release planning time and spikes, referred to as sprint zero. Features developed during those sprints go through Alpha-Beta gates, where performance metrics are closely monitored and assessed before making them generally available - usually at the end of a release.

A scrum team could comprise upto 9 members, including a product owner, designer, scrum master, devs, and QA.


2. The Baseline: Separate Tracks to the Same Destination

Building a typical feature followed a familiar pattern. Product managers spent several hours, sometimes days, in research, writing PRDs and user stories with acceptance criteria. Designers added in design links once stories were created.

Engineering translated the requirements into code and often found gaps mid-build, which meant conversations with Product and Design. Yes, this despite spikes, grooming, and planning sessions. Documentation (PRRs, TRRs, and others) happened at the end, once QA was complete.

Feature A was a 7-story-point feature, estimated at 7 working days of 8 hours each (56 hours), and the APIs it needed were already in place. To compare like for like, we rebuilt the baseline stage by stage across the lifecycle for similar features built conventionally.

3. Experiment 1: The Foundation (Feature A)

The first experiment kept the focus on the developer. The question was narrow: could AI take on pattern-based development? Working with Windsurf, the developer built all of Feature A in an estimated 13 hours across 2 days, against a forecast of 56 hours for development work had it been manually implemented.

The 13-hour breakdown below follows the developer's own estimate and distribution of time, not a time log, so it shows where the effort went rather than a measured split.

The key learning was that AI works best when it can follow patterns already in the codebase. It was not creating from scratch but learning our standards and our way of building. First-pass output typically needed 20-30% human refinement, so the developer's role leaned toward reviewing and steering.

What it showed, and what it forced next. Engineering had accelerated, but Product and Design were still working conventionally. That led directly to Experiment 2.

4. Experiment 2: Expanding Upstream (Feature B)

For the second experiment the question moved upstream: could AI help Product work faster too?

Using LLMs, I changed how I produced requirements. I built a prompt around a template and two reference examples of what good looks like, then iterated on it until the output carried enough detail for scalable usage. Along the way I improved the structure, boundaries and style of the stories so downstream teams could also use them for testing. I stored the finished prompt as a reusable custom project, so for each new feature I only had to add context.

Prompts were built and tested prior to experiments.

The PRD became the input for creating user stories and acceptance criteria. Everything was documented in Confluence and pushed to Jira via the embedded shortcut. All work post generation was manual.

What it showed, and what it forced next. We had now accelerated Product and Engineering independently. Design still sat in its own lane and the outputs were still handed across by copy-and-paste. That is what Experiment 3 set out to change.

Until this point, neither the Figma MCP nor the Atlassian MCP was available to us. Once access opened up, the developer and I set up connections to Figma and Atlassian.

On the design side, designers began using Figma Make to prototype and to find the best workflows to guide their designs early on, which cut iteration time by about 50% according to their estimates. Marvin was also introduced to condense disparate user feedback into insights.

5. Experiment 3: The Expressway (Feature C with MCPs)

The third experiment was different. We did not only want to move faster by building a unified route between our processes. Model Context Protocol (MCP) let us connect AI tools directly to the platforms we already worked in: LLMs, Confluence, Jira, Figma, Marvin, and Windsurf amongst many others. This feature was also more complicated than the previous two, with an estimate of just over a sprint. Having seen the gains from the first two experiments, we were optimistic it would be completed ahead of time.

  • Design. Designers could prototype about 50% faster, which also brought the handover to Engineering forward.

  • Figma MCP link. Developers pulled the designs straight into Windsurf instead of pasting the details in by hand, as they had in the earlier runs.

  • Atlassian MCP for technical documents. Developers used it to write and update technical documents for reference and review, which cut their TRR preparation time by about 50%.

  • PRR documentation. Product extended the LLMs to build the PRR documentation and presentation content, saving more time downstream.

  • Atlassian MCP link. Product exported output directly from LLMs to Confluence and Jira independently.

  • Design & LLMs. Designers began to use LLMs to push their contribution to Confluence and Jira.

Feature C’s development forecast was 72 hours, 11.5 hours more than the 60.5 hours of development capacity allotted to the previous experiments. With AI, development took an estimated 17 hours (just over 2 working days), a saving of 55 hours (76.4%). More important to me than the speed was the shape of the work. Product, Engineering and Design were contributing to one shared stream instead of hand-offs are various points.

What it showed. Integration worked wherever a tool exposed an MCP. The one place it fell short was the story push: when stories went from Confluence to Jira or straight from the LLM to Jira, some fields were erroneously populated, which meant some manual workaround was necessary.

Beyond Handoffs: Cross-Functional Validation

In previous sprint reviews, we occasionally identified discrepancies in design, eventing and acceptance-criteria coverage. These gaps could originate from any discipline. Product might overlook a condition when defining the acceptance criteria, Design might miss a component or workflow, or Engineering might fail to implement a documented requirement.

The downstream consequences depended on where the omission originated. A condition missed by Product could carry through to Design and Engineering without being represented in either output. Conversely, a requirement documented by Product but overlooked by Design or Engineering could create a discrepancy between the source requirements and the delivered feature.

In Experiment 3, MCP-connected access to Jira and Figma enabled the developer to cross-check implementation output against the acceptance criteria and designs. This introduced an additional validation activity within development, rather than relying solely on discrepancies being identified during sprint review.

The value was not simply faster access to information. The connected workflow enabled comparisons between documented intent, design and implementation, creating opportunities to identify mismatches earlier.

There was, however, an important limitation. These checks could identify discrepancies against documented requirements and designs, but they could not reliably identify a requirement that Product had never captured. An incomplete source requirement could still propagate through the workflow without producing an obvious mismatch.

We did not independently quantify the number of discrepancies prevented or the reduction in rework attributable to these checks. Nevertheless, the experiment demonstrated that MCP-connected source access could support validation as part of the AI-assisted development process, extending its utility beyond reducing manual handoffs.

6. Beyond Delivery: Two More Uses of the Tooling

The delivery experiments left two questions. Could the same way of working help decide what to build, and could it carry a feature through to its users building GTM content along the way? Both were independent projects. I include them because they show the tooling working at both ends of the delivery lifecycle.

6.1 Discovery: deciding what to build

The Clinical Inbox had gathered 150+ user-submitted feature ideas over nine years (2016 to 2025), with 5,500+ votes between them. All of which had not been addressed. The input was a de-identified CSV file from the customer success and voice-of-the-customer teams, which I gave to the LLMs to organise, structure, and turn into a story through brainstorming.

Instead of applying a fixed framework, I worked through the data with AI, asking one question at a time and letting each answer shape the next.

  1. What are users actually asking for? Long-form feedback was broken into individual statements and grouped into themes.

  2. How much do users care? Votes and engagement intensity showed which themes carried the most weight.

  3. How long have they been waiting? Many of the most-voted ideas were years old and still attracting votes.

  4. Who is affected, individuals or teams? This produced the most useful metric of the analysis, votes per account, which separates team-wide problems from individual preferences.

  5. Does priority change with perspective? Changing anchors to rank themes identified which themes held up consistently.

The outcome was a set of tiers. Each theme was then rated Critical, Important or Consider by anchoring tables on a specific measure to observe rankings. Themes that surfaced consistently as Critical, became the categories to focus on first.

A word of caution. The grouping involved my judgement, and a different analyst might group some ideas differently.

6.2 GTM: a byproduct of the PRD

A PRD already holds most of what a launch needs: who the feature is for, what it does and why it matters. For a new feature on mobile, I used the PRD as the source for a full communication plan.

With a chain-of-prompts using the attached context, brand guidelines set as guardrails, and examples of marketing-approved assets, the LLM was able to create a first draft that was acceptable.

The goal was to cut a sprint to 30% of its length (24 of 80 hours, about 3 days). QA, ceremonies and coordination (19.5 hours per sprint) did not compress with AI in Features A and B, while TRR documentation was only fixed in Feature C. It is possible that, if addressed by AI, these interactions and events could be compressed further.

An experiment trimming a release planning ritual by 18 hours was successfully conducted later ("Death by Grooming" - A Quarterly Ritual). Similar initiatives could be explored here.

8. What Changed for Each Role

Across three features and three experiments, AI did not only make us faster. It changed what each of us spent our time on.

  • Product. The mechanical work of writing comprehensive PRDs and stories moved to AI, which freed me to focus on user problems, clinical workflows, research, analytics, and the roadmap. The role moved from documentation toward strategy and judgement.

  • Engineering. With AI handling code generation, testing, and standard implementations, the developer's attention moved from boilerplate to architecture, performance and integration challenges.

  • Design. Designers were able to funnel comprehensive information into their designs, identify spots that weren't immediately obvious, and prototype quickly before investing time in iterations.

9. What I Learned

  1. Pattern recognition is AI's superpower. AI replicates patterns well. The better your existing patterns, documentation and architectural standards, the more useful it becomes, and effective prompting is critical.

  2. The connections matter as much as the capabilities. Experiments 1 and 2 each accelerated a lane in isolation. Experiment 3 is where the lanes shared one pool, each posting and pulling from the same place, and context stopped getting lost between them. Every discipline saw its own time fall, and stitched together those reductions bring the time to build a feature down by about half.

  3. Quality needs human oversight. Devs reported that first-pass output was typically 70-80% complete. The remaining 20-30% needed refinement, architectural judgement and validation. Budget about 30% of the time for iteration and you will still finish far faster. Similar observations were stated by Product and Design.

  4. Roles transform; they do not disappear. Nobody was replaced. The work shifted to the parts that need judgement.

  5. MCPs removed translation loss. Connections meant the code was built from the actual requirements and design specs, not from a retelling or repeated referencing of them.

10. Limits: What These Numbers Do Not Show

The headline is encouraging, and I would not want it read more strongly than the evidence allows.

  • One team, three features. The same one product manager, one developer and one designer worked on all three features, across every experiment, on one platform (Android). I cannot say how this transfers to other teams or to more complex features.

  • Estimates, not instrumented measurements. All hours are estimates. Feature A was used as the benchmark for the lifecycle table, and gains in Features B and C were estimated as we scaled AI use and MCP integration.

  • Not end to end. All Features had cleared QA, with defect rates within standard acceptance rates and priority 4 tags only. Later, QA used product acceptance criteria to build automated scripts for testing. Estimates were not available at the time of this paper.

  • Dependence on prompting skill and existing patterns. The gains came with a well-documented codebase and deliberate prompting. A team without those should expect less.

  • Asynchronous workflows. Product and Design don't often work in a linear process with development. They usually have plans laid out ahead of sprint zero, which means that engineering consumes pre-existing work when they head into development. Product and Design may only communicate intent and expectations and incorporate adjustments, if any, following dev input.

11. Conclusion

We started by asking whether AI could accelerate how we build features. It can, but that is not the important finding. Each experiment exposed the next constraint: first the speed of engineering, then the disconnected lanes, then the places where the connections themselves broke. By the third experiment, Product, Engineering and Design were contributing to one AI-augmented stream, reducing process time and context was no longer lost at each handoff.

That pattern is the idea I would most want to leave with you, and it echoes Andrew Ng's point that AI shifts the bottleneck from building to what to build next. When AI speeds up one part of the lifecycle, the bottleneck does not disappear; it moves to the next slowest link in the chain. Compress development, and the constraint moves upstream to Product and Design. Compress those, and it moves to the GTM work that precedes deployment. Every function next to an accelerated one has to compress with it, or it becomes the new constraint, until the whole system is set up for building in an AI world.

At the time of this writing, all three features were complete and had cleared QA, with two at the alpha gate. We have shown the route can be built, not that it is proven at scale.

The question is no longer whether AI can accelerate building. It is how fast we can go, now that we know the route exists. More importantly, it tells us to be observant of where the bottleneck shifts and the impact fixing it has on other upstream and downstream activities.
